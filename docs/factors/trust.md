# Trust

## Definition

Trust is whether people believe the knowledge enough to act on it without checking with someone first. It depends on accuracy, but accuracy isn't enough. People also need signals that tell them the content is current, owned, and authoritative.

Trust is difficult to earn and easy to lose. One wrong runbook during an incident can teach an entire team to ignore the wiki for a year.

## What zero looks like

- People find the right page and then confirm it with a colleague anyway.
- Multiple versions of the same document exist, and nobody knows which one is current.
- Pages have no owner, no last-reviewed date, or both.
- Teams keep private copies of shared documentation because they don't trust the shared version.
- AI assistants give answers that sound right and quote retired policies.

## Structural causes

Trust almost always collapses because of ownership, not writing. Most wiki failures aren't technology failures. They're ownership failures.

When nobody owns a page, nobody reviews it. When nobody reviews it, it drifts. When it drifts, people stop believing everything near it, including the pages that are still right. Distrust spreads faster than staleness does.

The second cause is the absence of authority. If three teams publish overlapping guidance and nothing marks one as canonical, readers default to asking a person.

## Diagnostic questions

1. What share of your top 100 pages has a named owner who still works here?
2. What share has a review date in the last 12 months?
3. Pick one critical process. How many documents describe it, and do they agree?
4. When an engineer finds a runbook during an incident, do they run it or ask someone first?
5. Has anyone been burned by outdated documentation in the last quarter? How widely did that story travel?

## What moves it

- Assign a named owner to every page that matters, and make ownership part of someone's actual job.
- Show the owner and last-reviewed date on the page itself.
- Mark canonical sources and retire or redirect duplicates.
- Set review cycles based on risk, not on a blanket schedule.
- Archive aggressively. Deleting a stale page raises trust in everything left behind.

Trust is usually the cheapest factor to improve and the one most often ignored. It doesn't need new content. It needs governance.
